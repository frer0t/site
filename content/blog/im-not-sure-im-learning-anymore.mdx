## the actual thing

it's not that coding got harder. it's that most of the time, i'm not the one doing it anymore. i describe what i want, something else writes it, and i review it. that's the job now for a lot of what used to be the fun part.

i keep waiting to feel relieved about that. mostly, i feel like i'm forgetting things i used to know cold.

## the part i haven't said out loud until now

a good chunk of what i shipped this year wasn't really mine, line by line. i read it, adjusted it, and signed off when it looked right, but the actual sentences, the structure, the part where you sit down and write the code — mostly happened somewhere else.

## it's not just the writing

building this blog feature is where it goes further. when i decided i wanted a real blog instead of a waitlist page, the old me would have spent an evening searching "best way to do markdown blog in next.js," reading three outdated tutorials, and picking something myself.

that's the part that actually gets to me. it's not that i can't do that kind of research anymore, it's that when the answer shows up this fast, there's no real reason to. the slow version, comparing a handful of libraries, reading through their issues, getting it wrong twice before it clicked, was where most of the actual learning happened. i still ended up with a tool called velite, something i'd genuinely never heard of before. i just didn't have to go find it myself.

it works great. that's not the problem. the problem is i can't tell anymore whether i'd have made a worse choice on my own, or a better one, because i never actually tried.

> if the thing that used to teach you how to think can now just do the thinking for you, what exactly are you practicing?

i don't have a tidy answer to that.

## what i know isn't true

it's not that ai is "bad" or that using it is cheating. that's too easy, and it's not actually what's bothering me.

it's not that i've stopped being able to code. i haven't.

it's not really about the output. the output is fine, and often better than what i'd have written alone.

## what i think is actually true

i learned this job by being confused for long stretches and fighting through it. that friction was annoying at the time, but looking back, it's where almost everything i actually know came from. a lot of that friction is optional now. i keep opting out of it, and i notice the cost later in some review or conversation where i realize i don't understand something as deeply as i used to expect myself to.

## where this leaves me

i'm not quitting, and i'm not about to write a post next month about how i "found the balance." i don't think i have. what i'm doing instead, for now, is small and unglamorous: on some problems, deliberately, i close the assistant and do it slowly and badly by hand, the exact same way i would have three years ago.

not because it's more productive. because i think it's the only part of this i still get to keep for myself.
